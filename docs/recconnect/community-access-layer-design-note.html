Rec Reporting · Design Note

The Denominator
Problem

Every report we ship divides by our own customers. Reno is asking a question that can only be answered by dividing by the population — and the data to do it is already in the database.

Prompted by Reno demo, 4 Sep 2026 Figures measured against prod, 5 Sep 2026
The ask

What Elena and Roabel actually wanted

Roabel's framing on the call: “how many people who are on 80% scholarships are actually utilizing their memberships… and getting more granular insights on how he can present and communicate that up to city leadership.” Elena's answer was that Reno tracks none of it today — and that the reason is tooling, not appetite: “it could just be that we hadn't in the past because it wasn't something that was conveniently available to us.”

That is not a request for a membership list with a scholarship column bolted on. It is a request for a claim a parks director can make to a city council: we serve this city in proportion to how this city actually looks — and here is where we don't. Nothing on the platform can produce that sentence today, because every denominator we own is our own customer base.

The trap that would have sunk this on first read

Platform-wide, zip_code is populated on only 236,107 of 837,265 users — 28.2%. Read at that grain the whole idea looks unbuildable. The dilution is every account that never bought anything. And resolving a household's ZIP through household.owner_id gets only 87.4%, because 11,744 member households have no resolvable owner. Take the ZIP from any member of the household and it is 99.7%. The grain of the question decides the answer.

The idea

Flip the denominator

There is no income field anywhere in the schema, and there should not be — asking a family their income at a rec-center front desk is not a product we want to build. But 644 ZIP codes is a small enough dimension to attach the U.S. Census American Community Survey to: median household income, poverty rate, household count, age structure, all published per ZCTA.

That does two things. The lesser one is an income band per member household. The greater one is that Census hands us the count of households that exist in each ZIP — a denominator we have never had. Penetration becomes computable:

What we report today 412 member households in 89502. A number with no scale — is that good?
What Census makes possible 3.1% of the households in 89502 — against 14.2% two ZIPs over, in the higher-income one.
What the director says to council 4.6× the gap in reach between the wealthiest and poorest quartile of the city we serve.

Illustrative ZIPs and figures — the arithmetic is what is being shown, not Reno's real numbers, since Reno is not live yet.

Every existing report answers “how are our members doing?” This answers “who isn't a member, and is that pattern about money?” — which is the only version of the question a city council cares about, and the reason it is worth building rather than adding a column.

Architecture

One household model, many readers

The unit is the household, not the person, because that is what the schema already commits to: membership is keyed on household_id and carries no user id at all. Address is a household fact. Family composition is a household fact. Participation is per-person and rolls up.

Two decisions worth making now

Census data ships as a checked-in JSON file, not a database table. It is 644 rows, it changes once a year when ACS releases, and it needs no write access to a database we do not own — the same reasoning that keeps WIZARD_SOURCE_GRAIN in server.js. Versioned in git means a number that moves is a diff someone can read.

It reads base tables, not the materialized schema. Every table there has exactly one index and every read is a full seq scan — the pinned platform problem. The queries behind every figure on this page ran against base tables in 0.3–2.3 s platform-wide, unscoped. This layer does not inherit that ceiling, and should not be built where it would.

The layer

Six surfaces, each ending in something to do

The rule that separates this from a dashboard: every panel resolves to an action or a list of named households, never a figure to admire. A count with nowhere to go is the dead end this codebase keeps writing down.

1 · Access map

Penetration and median household income per ZIP, on one choropleth. Sorted by the residual: where reach is furthest below what income and population predict.

ActionWhere outreach, a satellite program or a subsidy allocation goes next quarter.

2 · Subsidy → utilization

Roabel's question, exactly. Of households on a discount or scholarship, what share ever checked in — and how their visit frequency compares with full-pay households on the same plan.

ActionThe named list of awards that were issued and never used — a phone call, not a policy change.

3 · Life-stage gaps

Our age distribution against the Census age distribution of the same ZIPs. Surfaces the teen cliff, the young-adult hole, and senior reach as three separate findings rather than one chart.

ActionWhich age band to program or price for — sized, so the business case writes itself.

4 · Pricing fit

Household size against the tiers actually sold. Measured today: 49.1% of member households are one person and the mean is 1.99 — while orgs price “family” tiers for four.

ActionRetier. Pairs with the annual-vs-monthly finding already in the Memberships report.

5 · Conversion gaps

Households that register for programs but never buy a membership, and residency-group households that have never transacted — cut by income band, so the reason is legible.

ActionA segmented contact list, through the Community Intel export path that already exists.

6 · Price sensitivity

Conversion rate at each price point, banded by area income. Distinguishes a discount that unlocks new households from one that hands money to households who would have paid.

ActionSet the discount depth where it buys participation, and defend it with the elasticity.
Integrity

Four rules this cannot ship without

An area income is not a person's income

ZIP + ACS is an area-level proxy. A low-income family in an affluent ZIP reads affluent, and the inference is invalid at the individual row — the ecological fallacy, and it is the kind of error that ends up in a council presentation. So: income bands are reported at ZIP grain only, never rendered as a field on a household or a person, never exported next to a name. The label on screen says area median every time it appears. Separately, USPS ZIPs are delivery routes and Census ZCTAs are areal approximations of them — a real but small mismatch that the reference file should carry a flag for rather than paper over.

Suppress thin cells

A ZIP with six member households, cut by age and subsidy status, is not a statistic — it is a description of identifiable families. Any cell under a floor (start at 10 households) shows its count and refuses the percentage, the same rule already used for RATE_MIN_VIEWS and WL_CONV_MIN_OFFERS. A real 0% still shows; an unsafe one does not.

“Subsidized” has four spellings and no single field

Measured: 614 scholarship stored-value accounts across 19 orgs; 135,985 active memberships whose list price is already $0 (mostly residency records); 4,674 genuinely discounted off a real list price, worth $495,857 foregone; and 1,454 discount-code redemptions. One derived predicate has to unify them, and it must be presence-gated — an org that uses none of these mechanisms shows nothing, never a confident zero. Note also that where a group sets the price tier rather than discounting it, defaultCents equals finalCents and the true subsidy is understated; the group's own price ladder is the correction.

Absent, not zero

72% of member households have a date of birth, so 28% do not. Age panels report on the households that can answer and say how many those are — the hasAbsent rule. A denominator that includes households that cannot answer produces the “0.3 guests per booking” defect one report over. And guard the dates: 128 people in member households compute to an implausible age today.

Build

How it lands

Phased so each step is useful alone and nothing depends on Reno signing. It extends the Community Intel report rather than becoming a 23rd report type — that page is already the “who are our people” surface, and already carries the segmented-CSV and activity-beacon machinery this needs.

01

The model and the reference file

One card producing household_profile at org grain, plus zip_reference.json from ACS 5-year. No UI. Proven by an equivalence gate against the existing membership counts, so the new model cannot silently disagree with the report people already read.

Ships nothing visible. Everything after depends on it.
02

Access tab — the map and the ladder

Penetration by ZIP, income band, life-stage gaps. This is the council-slide surface and the one that demos.

Usable by every live org on day one — Apex, Norman, El Segundo.
03

Subsidy → utilization

Roabel's question, plus the never-activated award list. Wants an org running real subsidies at volume, which today means Reno — so this is the phase that follows the deal rather than leading it.

Gated on the unified subsidy predicate from phase 1.
04

Opportunity engine

Ranked, written findings — “89502 is 3.1% penetrated against 14.2% citywide; 1,100 households, median income $41k; nearest facility 4 miles” — each with its contact list attached. The AI writes the prose, the page writes the numbers, exactly as the wizard split already works.

Open

Decisions I'd want from you

Who is allowed to see it? Merging area income with age and address is a materially different product from a rental schedule, even aggregated. My instinct is the reportSettings shape — super-admin gated behind a flag, per-org opt-in, and the export separately gated from the view.
Does Rec model RecConnect as a group, a $0 membership, or a scholarship? It decides whether the tier is queryable per household at all. Groups do not expire — the thing Elena asked about and the thing her annual re-up depends on — so the cohort would have no time dimension. This gets settled in configuration long before reporting sees it, and reporting should have a say.
Is this a Rec product or a Reno deliverable? Built generically it is a differentiator in every muni RFP that asks about equity — and most now do. Built for Reno it is faster and lands in one quarter. The measurements say generic costs little more, because the fields are populated platform-wide, not just for one org.
How much Census do we take? Income and household counts alone is a small, defensible file. Adding race, language and disability from the same tables makes the equity story far stronger and the privacy conversation far harder. I would start with income, population and age, and treat the rest as a separate decision with legal in the room.
